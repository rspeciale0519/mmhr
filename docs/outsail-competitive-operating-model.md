# OutSail competitive operating model

Research date: 2026-08-06
Scope: public marketing site plus the prior fresh-account product audit
Purpose: identify the operating system MatchMaker HR should emulate without
copying OutSail's protected copy, media, or brand expression

## Executive conclusion

OutSail is best understood as a **two-sided software brokerage with a guided
buying workspace**, not as a standalone matching algorithm.

Its buyer-facing promise is a free, structured path from current-state audit to
vendor selection. Its economic engine sits on the supply side: the winning HR
software vendor pays a commission after selection. OutSail says more than 220
vendors participate under a consistent rate model. That enables a free core
offer, high-volume buyer acquisition, and a proprietary data flywheel around
pricing, fit, evaluation outcomes, and sales behavior.

The product and go-to-market system has five reinforcing parts:

1. Attract buyers through search content, case studies, brokers, and PE/VC
   channels.
2. Convert them with a no-fee audit and selection workflow.
3. Collect structured current-state, complexity, team, and buying data.
4. Guide a vendor selection and receive a commission from the winner.
5. Feed each selection's pricing and outcome evidence back into future advice,
   content, and proof.

OutSail also sells a deeper **Elevate** advisory tier starting at **$30,000**
for complex projects. The free **Select** tier is therefore both a real product
and the top of a marketplace/advisory funnel.

### The advisory layer

Human expertise is designed into the product rather than positioned as an
unrelated sales add-on. The public service taxonomy mirrors the software
workflow:

1. Tech-stack audit
2. Requirements building
3. Vendor shortlisting
4. RFP and vendor selection
5. Proposal analysis and negotiation
6. Business-case development

This gives OutSail an important operational advantage: automation can structure
the work while advisors handle ambiguity, political alignment, vendor nuance,
and negotiation. The product gathers the context that makes the advisor call
high leverage.

## Economics and incentives

### Free buyer tier

OutSail Select is marketed as free from initial audit through final selection.
It includes a tech-stack audit, one advisory session, scorecards, a demo
debrief, pre-built questions, a shortlist report, sales-rep note review,
negotiation guidance, basic proposal analysis, a business-case template, and an
implementation-readiness assessment.

### Paid complexity tier

OutSail Elevate starts at $30,000 and adds role-specific intake, a custom plan,
requirements blueprint, consensus and executive alignment, integration
mapping, expert scorecard setup, vendor management, unlimited debriefs,
tailored demo agendas, deeper proposal comparison, a polished business case,
and implementation handoff. A stay-versus-go analysis is an add-on.

Primary evidence: [pricing](temp/outsail-site-2026-08-06/pages/pricing/content.md).

### Vendor-funded brokerage

OutSail says it is paid by the winning vendor after a client completes an
evaluation. It claims all major vendors pay the same commission rate so its
economics do not change based on which vendor wins. Its vendor pitch is:

- no upfront launch investment;
- a referral agreement plus enablement on ideal customer profile and
  differentiators;
- better-prepared buyers;
- higher completion rates;
- sales cycles shortened by structured requirements and evaluation tools.

Primary evidence:
[broker model](temp/outsail-site-2026-08-06/pages/software-broker-model/content.md)
and [vendor partnership](temp/outsail-site-2026-08-06/pages/software-vendors/content.md).

### Post-purchase extension

OutSail says it connects clients to vetted implementation and optimization
partners experienced with the selected platform. This extends the relationship
without requiring OutSail to build a full implementation-services bench.

## Data flywheel and moat

The broker model creates a compounding data advantage if the claims are borne
out operationally:

```text
Free buyer workflow
  -> more structured selections
  -> more pricing, fit, objection, and outcome data
  -> better shortlists and negotiation context
  -> better buyer outcomes and vendor conversion
  -> stronger case studies and vendor participation
  -> more buyer demand
```

The true moat is not the questionnaire UI. It is the combination of:

- standardized buyer requirements;
- vendor capability and ideal-customer profiles;
- current commercial/pricing evidence;
- evaluation and loss-reason data;
- advisor judgment;
- post-selection outcome feedback;
- repeat distribution through brokers and investment firms.

## Marketing promise versus observed product

The prior fresh-account audit found a serious activation gap.

The questionnaire did capture rich commercial context: primary vendor, module
coverage, 15 satisfaction dimensions, operational complexity, automation,
team size and maturity, and external support. It exposed a 132-vendor catalog.
However, the observed completion result did not visibly deliver the promised
gaps, redundancies, benchmarks, recommendations, risks, or action plan. The
point-solution step exposed no visible add interaction. Workspace, tasks,
vendors, and scores stayed gated until an advisor session was booked.

This suggests the audit currently functions at least partly as:

- structured lead qualification;
- advisor preparation;
- commitment/intent creation;
- a productized handoff into human curation.

That is commercially rational, but it risks breaking trust when a user invests
substantial effort and receives little immediate diagnostic value.

Primary evidence:
[questionnaire audit](temp/outsail-questionnaire-comprehensive-audit.md).

## What MatchMaker HR should emulate

1. **A lifecycle, not a quiz.** Connect audit, market research, evaluation,
   selection, and implementation readiness in one data model.
2. **Immediate structure.** Turn vague pain into a visible stack, requirements,
   decision criteria, and owner-ready artifacts.
3. **Shared evaluation discipline.** Use weighted scorecards, consistent demo
   questions, live notes, and stakeholder scoring.
4. **Commercial intelligence.** Normalize proposals and expose assumptions,
   fees, benchmarks, and negotiation targets.
5. **Human-plus-software delivery.** Let software gather and organize evidence;
   let experts resolve nuance and align stakeholders.
6. **Partner-led distribution.** Give benefit brokers and PE/VC teams a
   credible capability they can repeatedly bring to clients and portfolio
   companies.
7. **Outcome-oriented proof.** Build case studies around decision time, avoided
   spend, negotiated savings, implementation risk, and operational improvement.
8. **A compounding data model.** Capture why vendors fit, why they lose, what
   they cost, what was negotiated, and whether the decision worked.
9. **Transparent economics.** Explain exactly who pays, when, and how conflicts
   are controlled.
10. **A paid complexity tier.** Keep a useful self-service/core experience while
    charging for bespoke discovery, stakeholder alignment, project management,
    and executive-grade deliverables.

## What MatchMaker HR should improve

1. **Deliver value before the advisor gate.** Completion should produce a real
   diagnostic, not just a booking CTA.
2. **Make stack building first-class.** Users need obvious add/edit/remove flows
   for every suite and point solution.
3. **Reflect every important answer back.** Show users how an input changed the
   assessment or recommendation.
4. **Support review and correction.** Add a final review, post-completion edits,
   exports, and data-confidence labels.
5. **Separate evidence from judgment.** Each recommendation should show why it
   fits, why it might not, source freshness, and confidence.
6. **Prove neutrality operationally.** Equal commission alone does not remove
   every incentive. Publish eligibility rules, rate policy, advisor controls,
   excluded vendors, and audit logs.
7. **Avoid a thin content moat.** Hundreds of SEO pages are useful only when
   they stay accurate. Product telemetry, pricing evidence, and client outcomes
   should drive the content system.
8. **Treat accessibility and data validation as product quality.** The prior
   audit found slider/accessibility risk, permissive vendor entry, hidden
   defaults, and repetitive team-member entry.

## Clean-room implementation blueprint

### Phase 1 — Diagnostic that earns trust

Build a guided intake covering:

- project intent and decision horizon;
- current suite and point solutions;
- capability coverage and redundancy;
- integrations and manual handoffs;
- process complexity and automation;
- team capacity, seniority, tenure, and expertise;
- pain, risk, budget, and stakeholder readiness.

The immediate result must include:

1. a current-state stack map;
2. a summary of what the user said;
3. capability gaps and potential redundancies;
4. risk and readiness assessment;
5. prioritized next actions;
6. an initial vendor archetype or shortlist direction;
7. confidence and methodology.

Offer the advisor as “review this diagnostic with an expert,” not “book to see
your result.”

### Phase 2 — Vendor intelligence

Create structured vendor profiles with capability coverage, ideal-customer
profile, employee-size range, geography, integrations, implementation model,
service model, pricing bands, strengths, limitations, evidence dates, and
source confidence. Start with a narrow, well-maintained market rather than
claiming exhaustive coverage early.

### Phase 3 — Collaborative evaluation

Add shortlists, stakeholder roles, weighted scorecards, demo agendas,
requirement-linked notes, scoring calibration, consensus views, risks, and an
explainable recommendation record.

### Phase 4 — Commercial decision

Add proposal normalization, total-cost modeling, benchmark ranges, contract
assumptions, negotiation targets, reference tracking, approval-ready business
cases, and implementation-readiness handoff.

### Phase 5 — Marketplace and data flywheel

Only after buyer trust exists, introduce vendor referral economics and partner
channels. Required controls should include:

- a published commission policy;
- equal-rate or clearly disclosed rate bands;
- vendor inclusion/exclusion criteria;
- recommendation explanations independent of payout;
- conflict disclosures at the point of recommendation;
- an outcome-feedback loop after implementation.

## Recommended near-term priorities for MMHR

| Priority | Build | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Now | stack builder + immediate diagnostic | converts intake effort into trust and differentiates from OutSail's observed gap |
| Now | transparent recommendation explanation | establishes credibility before marketplace economics exist |
| Next | vendor profile schema + evidence freshness | foundation for matching and eventual pricing intelligence |
| Next | shortlist and shared scorecard | turns a one-time result into a collaborative workflow |
| Next | advisor review workflow | monetizes expertise without withholding the core result |
| Later | proposal normalization and business case | highest leverage after users reach finalist stage |
| Later | broker/PE partner portal | repeat distribution once delivery quality is proven |
| Later | vendor-funded model | powerful, but only after neutrality governance and sufficient vendor coverage |

## Bottom line

The piece worth mimicking is OutSail's **system**: free structured buying,
expert augmentation, supply-side monetization, partner distribution, and a
selection-data flywheel. The competitive opening is to make the software
itself deliver the diagnostic value it promises, explain every recommendation,
and treat the advisor as acceleration rather than a gate.
